Fun facts

Machine About Fun facts

Each of these can be checked against the machine on the front page — most of them in under a minute.

  1. A letter could never encode to itself

    The reflector guarantees it: the signal always returns by a different wire. Type AAAA with the default machine and you will get four letters, none of them A. Codebreakers used that as a first filter, and it is why a guessed word could be placed against a ciphertext at all.

  2. The middle rotor double-steps

    Set the rotors to ADU and press three times: the window runs ADV, AEW, BFX. The middle rotor carried the left-hand rotor, then stepped again on its own notch. Nobody designed it that way on purpose and every simulation has to reproduce it.

  3. More plugboard cables meant fewer keys

    Ten cables give 150,738,274,937,250 arrangements; thirteen give fewer. The count peaks at ten or eleven and then falls, because each extra cable also removes choices. The key-space panel lets you drag a slider and watch it happen.

  4. The key space was never the weak point

    A Wehrmacht Enigma I had about 2.79 × 1024 keys — 81 bits, beyond exhaustive search by any machine of the era. It was broken through procedure: predictable keys, lazy operators, and a repeated indicator.

  5. The message key was sent twice

    The operator set the rotors to the daily key, encrypted a three-letter message key twice, and sent those six letters as a header. The same letters, six positions apart, under the same start — a gift to anyone with enough intercepts. The signal panel on the front page shows the six letters.

  6. The Poles broke it first, in 1932

    Marian Rejewski reconstructed the military rotor wiring from intercepted indicators using group theory, before the war. Jerzy Różycki's clock method and Henryk Zygalski's perforated sheets followed. Britain got the results at a meeting outside Warsaw in July 1939, weeks before the invasion.

  7. The naval rotors had two notches

    Rotors VI, VII and VIII turn over at both Z and M, so they advance the neighbouring rotor twice as often as an army rotor. It made the naval traffic harder in a way that has nothing to do with key length.

  8. M4 added a rotor that never turns

    From February 1942 the U-boats used a fourth, thin rotor (Beta or Gamma) in the leftmost slot. It has a ring and wiring but no notch, so it stays still while the other three work. It multiplied the key space and plunged Bletchley Park into a blackout for most of that year.

  9. Operators had a mnemonic for the notches

    The letters visible just before turnover — Q, E, V, J, Z for rotors I to V — were remembered as Royal Flags Wave Kings Above. The offset by one letter is because the rotors step before the lamp lights.

  10. It was a commercial product first

    Arthur Scherbius patented a cipher machine in 1918 and sold Enigma machines to businesses and railways before any army bought one. The military version added the plugboard, which is where most of the key space comes from.

  11. Ring settings and window letters are not the same thing

    The ring setting rotates the wiring against the alphabet ring; the window position rotates the whole rotor. Change one and the notch moves relative to the letters you see. Two operators with the same window letters and different ring settings are running different ciphers.

  12. You can build a machine that never existed

    The builder goes up to a hundred rotors with your own notches, your own alphabet and seeded wiring. The key space becomes absurd — and it still does not help, because the weakness was never the arithmetic.
